# Research and Co-Creation

Use this file before writing research plans, recruiting participants, facilitating inclusive workshops, running co-design sprints, or testing prototypes. It focuses on the operational details that sit below the core skill: activity workflows, session structure, recruiting objectives, screeners, and facilitation prompts.

This is not a substitute for specialist research ethics, legal accessibility review, clinical mental-health guidance, or medical diagnosis. It is a practical guide for designing research that learns from people with lived experience and turns that learning into product decisions.

## What This File Produces

Use the guidance here to produce one or more of the following:

- A research objective framed by motivation, goal, task, cognitive demand, and likely mismatch
- A co-design sprint agenda
- A participant recruitment profile or screener
- A moderated interview guide
- A workshop facilitation script
- A prototype test plan
- A synthesis board structure
- A prioritized set of inclusive design opportunities
- A traceable decision log connecting participant insights to product changes

## Research Operating Rules

Co-creation is not a late-stage usability test. Bring people with lived experience into the work early enough to change the problem framing, not merely approve an almost-finished solution.

Treat participants as subject-matter experts in the barriers they experience. The product team brings design, engineering, and product judgment; participants bring lived expertise, adaptations, context, and evidence of what currently fails.

Do not recruit only by diagnosis. Recruit by need spectrum, cognitive demand, context, and mismatch. A person with ADHD who does not struggle with interruptions may be less relevant to a focus-interruption study than a person without ADHD who loses hours after being interrupted.

Do not rely on empathy simulations alone. Simulations can reveal friction in a prototype, but they cannot reproduce the emotional, social, economic, cultural, or long-term context of disability.

Do not ask people to share sensitive health information unless it is necessary, consented, and handled appropriately. Most product research can be framed around experiences, preferences, demands, and barriers instead of diagnoses.

Design the session itself inclusively: clear expectations, accessible materials, multiple participation modes, breaks, plain language, predictable timing, opt-out moments, compensation, privacy clarity, and a moderator who can slow down when needed.

## Before Any Research Plan

Complete this framing pass first.

1. Product or flow: name the experience under study.
2. Human motivation: identify the broader motivation, such as learning, creating, connecting, succeeding, staying safe, feeling prepared, completing work, finding support, or reducing uncertainty.
3. Goal: name the outcome the person wants.
4. Task: name the concrete action sequence the product asks them to complete.
5. Cognitive demands: mark learning, focus, decision-making, recall, communication, or a combination.
6. Emotional demands: note stress, anxiety, shame, urgency, low confidence, fear of making a mistake, privacy concern, sensory overload, or task paralysis.
7. Mismatches: list where the current experience assumes ability, memory, attention, language, confidence, time, money, social support, device access, or technical expertise.
8. Contexts: name physical, social, environmental, bandwidth, device, assistive technology, cultural, and time-pressure conditions.
9. Decision needed: state what product decision this research must inform.
10. Risk of not researching: state what will likely be designed from assumption if this work is skipped.

## Research Objective Template

Use this structure:

`We need to understand how people who experience [need spectrum / mismatch] accomplish [task] in order to support [motivation and goal] without increasing [cognitive, emotional, physical, social, or privacy demand]. The research will inform [specific product decisions].`

Examples:

`We need to understand how people who struggle to recover after interruptions evaluate in-product suggestions while writing code, in order to support flow and discovery without increasing focus demand. The research will inform timing, placement, dismissibility, and customization of suggestions.`

`We need to understand how people with low confidence using new software start a survey-creation flow, in order to support learning and decision-making without causing avoidance or self-blame. The research will inform template selection, guidance, progress feedback, and error handling.`

`We need to understand how people using assistive technology and adaptive input devices complete account setup, in order to support access without requiring hidden workarounds. The research will inform form structure, device compatibility, guidance, and support content.`

## Co-Design Sprint Models

Use the sprint length that matches the decision at stake. Shorter sprints can surface mismatches and priorities. Longer sprints are better for ideation, prototype iteration, and stakeholder alignment.

### 90-Minute Discovery Sprint

Use when the team needs fast orientation before a research plan or concept review.

1. Welcome, access check, consent, and agenda: 10 minutes
2. Product context and decision to inform: 5 minutes
3. Participant stories and adaptations: 25 minutes
4. Mismatch mapping: 20 minutes
5. Persona spectrum or need spectrum mapping: 15 minutes
6. Opportunity questions: 10 minutes
7. Close, compensation reminder, and feedback-loop expectation: 5 minutes

Output: top mismatches, initial participant profile, research risks, and 3-5 opportunity questions.

### Half-Day Co-Creation Sprint

Use when a product team has a concept, prototype, or flow but can still change direction.

1. Welcome, access check, consent, session norms: 20 minutes
2. Motivation-goal-task framing: 20 minutes
3. Learn from experts interviews or story rounds: 45 minutes
4. Break: 15 minutes
5. Capture research insights: 30 minutes
6. Persona spectrum or persona network: 30 minutes
7. Mismatch to Solution I: 30 minutes
8. Mismatch to Solution II: 45 minutes
9. Share concepts, risks, and open questions: 30 minutes
10. Close: 10 minutes

Output: refined problem framing, participant-informed opportunity questions, concept clusters, and prototype priorities.

### One-Day Co-Design Sprint

Use when the team needs to move from research insights to a testable prototype.

1. Welcome, access check, consent, expectations: 20 minutes
2. Human-to-computer role-play or computer trust: 30 minutes
3. Participant stories and adaptations: 60 minutes
4. Break: 15 minutes
5. Capture research insights: 40 minutes
6. Persona spectrum and context mapping: 40 minutes
7. Lunch or longer break: 45 minutes
8. Mismatch to Solution I and II: 75 minutes
9. Design a microinteraction: 45 minutes
10. Low-fidelity prototype: 60 minutes
11. Participant review and reflection: 35 minutes
12. Close and next-step commitment: 10 minutes

Output: prototypeable concept, microinteraction detail, unresolved risks, participant quotes, decision log, and follow-up test plan.

### Two-Day Co-Design Sprint

Use for major flows, AI behavior, device-adjacent experiences, high-risk accessibility concerns, or product-to-physical journeys.

Day 1: understand and frame.

1. Welcome, access check, consent, and psychological safety norms
2. Learn from experts interviews
3. Interaction diary review or contextual walkthrough
4. Capture research insights
5. Persona spectrum and persona network
6. Human analogy and role of technology
7. Mismatch to Solution I
8. End-of-day synthesis and participant check-in

Day 2: ideate, prototype, and stress-test.

1. Recap and correction of Day 1 synthesis
2. Mismatch to Solution II
3. Design a microinteraction
4. Low-fidelity prototype
5. Context and capability match
6. Situational adaptation
7. Prototype walkthrough and participant critique
8. Decision log, open risks, follow-up commitments

Output: revised journey map, need spectrum, concept shortlist, prototype, adaptation matrix, and product backlog items.

## Co-Design Team Roles

Minimum roles:

- Session lead: owns objective, pace, decision quality, and participant safety.
- Moderator: speaks with participants and facilitates the conversation.
- Access producer: manages accommodations, materials, captions, interpretation, room setup, remote access, breaks, and assistive technology needs.
- Note-taker: captures quotes, behaviors, adaptations, mismatches, and unresolved questions.
- Synthesis lead: turns session data into themes and product implications.
- Prototype lead: creates or modifies prototypes during or immediately after the session.
- Product decision owner: confirms which insights will affect roadmap, scope, and acceptance criteria.

Recommended observers:

- Engineering, content design, product management, research, accessibility, support, marketing, legal/privacy when data or AI is involved, and customer-facing roles.

Observer rule: observers listen and ask clarifying questions through the moderator. They do not debate participants, defend the product, or solution before the mismatch is understood.

## Participant Mix

A balanced inclusive study usually includes more than one participant type.

- Lived-experience experts: people who experience the target mismatch permanently or frequently.
- Adjacent participants: people who experience the same barrier temporarily or situationally.
- Current customers: people using the existing product or closest available alternative.
- New or low-familiarity participants: people who reveal onboarding, learning, and hidden-assumption failures.
- Assistive technology or adaptive setup users: people who use screen readers, magnification, captions, switch input, voice input, alternative keyboards, alternative pointers, braille displays, high contrast, display scaling, or custom physical configurations.
- Support network participants: caregivers, interpreters, colleagues, family members, or assistants when the task depends on another person.
- Internal stakeholders: people who need to understand the consequences of product decisions but should not replace external or lived-experience participants.

## Recruitment Rules

Use a screener only after the research objective is clear.

1. Choose no more than 3-4 cognitive spectrums total across the screener.
2. Include no more than 1-2 open-ended questions unless the study genuinely requires more.
3. Add mandatory demographic diversity requirements such as age, ethnicity, gender spectrum, income, education, language, physical ability, disability status, work mode, tech literacy, location, and access to time, money, or social support.
4. Ask about access needs before the session and treat them as logistics, not as a reason to exclude.
5. Include a pre-session conversation with potential co-creators to confirm fit, answer questions, identify accommodations, and reduce anxiety.
6. Recruit across the need spectrum, not only from named diagnoses.
7. Include people who do and do not use the product if the goal includes discovery, onboarding, or learning.
8. For mental-health-related research, screen for experience with stressors, emotional impact, confidence, control, and task difficulty; avoid asking for clinical details unless essential and ethically approved.
9. For AI research, screen for privacy comfort, personalization expectations, willingness to correct AI, and experience with recommendations or automation.
10. Compensate participants fairly and make payment logistics clear before participation.

## Screener Structure

Keep screeners short and purposeful.

### 1. Study Fit

- What product, task, or context are you studying?
- How often does the participant encounter that task or a close equivalent?
- Does the participant experience the target mismatch or need spectrum?
- Does the participant use relevant assistive technology, device configuration, or workaround?
- Does the participant have availability and consent to participate in the planned format?

### 2. Demographic and Context Mix

Ask only what is necessary to balance the participant pool. Consider:

- Age range
- Gender identity or gender spectrum, when relevant to inclusive sampling
- Race, ethnicity, culture, or language context
- Education and tech familiarity
- Income or access constraints when cost, subscriptions, bandwidth, devices, or support networks matter
- Work mode: in-person, hybrid, remote, shift work, caregiving, school, or unemployment
- Disability-related access needs or assistive technology use
- Device and browser environment
- Geographic and bandwidth context

### 3. Cognitive Demand Screen

Pick only the areas that map to the product task.

- Learn: how people approach unfamiliar tasks, guidance, and experimentation.
- Focus: how people handle interruptions, stimuli, task switching, and sustained attention.
- Decision-making: how much information, consequence visibility, and tradeoff clarity people need.
- Recall: how people remember steps, state, credentials, past decisions, and where they left off.
- Communication: how people prefer tone, format, timing, modality, and personalization.
- Self-efficacy: confidence with unfamiliar technology and problem-solving.
- Attitude toward risk: comfort with new features, updates, and uncertain outcomes.

### 4. Access and Session Needs

Ask directly and neutrally:

- What would make the session easier or more comfortable for you?
- Do you need captions, transcripts, interpretation, large text, high contrast materials, screen-reader-compatible materials, breaks, camera-off participation, written prompts in advance, alternative input, extra time, or another accommodation?
- Are there any topics, tasks, or formats you would prefer to avoid?
- Do you prefer to answer verbally, in writing, by demonstration, or through another format?

## Screener Question Bank

Use these as building blocks. Do not use every question.

### Learn

Use when the product requires onboarding, new-feature adoption, help content, tutorials, setup, configuration, or unfamiliar workflows.

Scale statement, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- My first step in learning new technology is experimenting and tinkering with it.

Multiple choice:

- How do you approach learning something new for the first time?
  - I like tinkering around and trying until I figure it out.
  - I want guidance before starting, such as how-to videos or documents.
  - I want structure and a lot of support, such as a class or an expert available.

Open-ended:

- If you were cooking a complicated new meal for the first time, what would you do?
- If you needed to learn something new on a computer, such as coding or advanced spreadsheet work, how would you approach it?
- If you had trouble with the touchpad on your laptop, how would you try to solve it?

Selection guidance: for guided-learning research, look for people who do not start by tinkering, who seek structured guidance, who ask experts, or who prefer tutorials before action.

### Focus

Use when the product includes notifications, suggestions, alerts, modals, badges, AI assistance, multitasking, dense information, collaboration, or long work sessions.

Multiple choice:

- What best describes how you feel about technology interruptions?
  - I require complete isolation to do my best work; interruptions are difficult to recover from.
  - When I am interrupted by product features, I can experience negative emotions like frustration or anxiety.
  - I do not mind technology interruptions if they are useful to me.
  - I do not mind technology interruptions; they do not negatively impact me.

Open-ended:

- How do you maintain sustained concentration while focusing on an important project?
- What alerts and interruptions appear in your day today? Which are disruptive, and which are helpful?

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- Task switching with technology is hard and takes me out of focus.
- I feel protective over my focus time and struggle to tune people and technology out.
- I struggle to tune interruptions out when I want to focus on one task for a long time.
- I struggle with selective attention, such as focusing on one person in a loud office.
- It is important for me to focus on one task for a long time without distraction.
- I have a hard time selecting what to pay attention to when there is a lot of stimuli around.
- I can easily ignore external and internal distractions.
- Technology notifications make focusing difficult.

Selection guidance: if the study targets interruption harm, exclude people who report no negative impact unless they are included as comparison participants.

### Decision-Making

Use when the product asks people to choose plans, settings, privacy options, irreversible actions, purchases, configuration, recommendations, or AI-generated suggestions.

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I spend a lot of time doing research before making important decisions.
- When a decision needs to be made, it is important to me to gather relevant details before deciding.
- I want to get things right the first time, so I gather as much information as I can before acting.
- I want to be aware of consequences before deciding or acting with technology.
- When there are too many steps or too much information, I get overwhelmed and often ask for help or avoid making a decision.
- I want to understand tradeoffs before choosing.

Selection guidance: include people who need consequences, tradeoffs, or detailed information before action, and people who avoid decisions when overloaded.

### Recall

Use when the product requires passwords, usernames, prior decisions, saved state, multi-step flows, interrupted work, recent documents, task resumption, or remembering where something is located.

Multiple choice:

- How often do you have trouble remembering where you put important items such as keys?
  - Always
  - Sometimes
  - Rarely
  - Never

Multiple choice:

- To remember where I put something important usually takes me:
  - More than 10 tries
  - 5-10 tries
  - 2-5 tries
  - 1 try

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I often reset my passwords and usernames.
- I write down my passwords and usernames.
- I use similar passwords and usernames to remember them.
- I use digital tools to help remember passwords and usernames.
- I remember passwords and usernames without assistance.

Open-ended:

- What assistance, if any, do you use to remember passwords, usernames, tasks, or where you left off?

Selection guidance: include people who use memory systems, reset often, rely on cues, or struggle after interruption.

### Communication

Use when the product includes help, alerts, AI assistants, coaching, summaries, feedback, tone adaptation, personalization, collaboration, or multimodal output.

Open-ended:

- How do you prefer technology to communicate important information such as tips, updates, suggestions, or guidance?
- What would you want technology, such as an AI assistant, to get to know about you over time to personalize an experience?

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I do not want technology to know me or use my information to personalize an experience.
- If I have used a technology multiple times and it does not remember or leverage information about me to make the experience easier, I get frustrated.
- I want technology to adapt to my communication preferences over time.
- I want technology to get to know me over time and use information about me to make my experience easier, faster, or better.
- I do not want technology to assume a communication preference without asking.

Selection guidance: include people with varied preferences across real-time pop-ups, quiet summaries, visual cues, written guidance, audio, multimodal communication, and explicit consent.

### Self-Efficacy

Use when confidence, task initiation, troubleshooting, or self-blame may affect participation.

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I can use unfamiliar technology features when I only have the internet as a reference.
- I am comfortable using technology.

Multiple choice:

- How would you approach a new challenge with technology?
  - It would feel like a fun puzzle; I would get started solving it.
  - It would feel challenging but possible.
  - Many challenges with technology feel impossible to me.

Open-ended:

- When there is a significant problem with your computer, how effective do you feel you can be at solving it yourself?

Selection guidance: include low-confidence participants when studying guidance, support, errors, new features, or task initiation.

### Attitude Toward Risk

Use when the product introduces new features, software updates, AI suggestions, automation, account changes, financial consequences, privacy choices, or unfamiliar workflows.

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I avoid using new apps or features before they are well tested.
- I worry that new features will make it harder to get my job done.
- I avoid new software features until I feel certain they will be useful.
- I enjoy trying new features even if I am not fully confident they will help.
- It is better to try a new feature and see that it does not work than to miss information that could be useful.
- I avoid running software updates because I worry an update will break something.
- I enjoy exploring unfamiliar features to see what they do.
- I enjoy finding lesser-known capabilities in the software I use.

Multiple choice:

- How do you feel about adopting new features?
  - I enjoy trying new features even if I am not fully confident they will help.
  - I avoid new software features until I feel certain they will be useful.

Selection guidance: include both risk-averse and exploratory participants when testing onboarding, release communication, default settings, or AI automation.

### Mental Health and Emotional Demand

Use when the task may involve stress, overwhelm, shame, uncertainty, deadlines, privacy anxiety, social perception, burnout, or low motivation.

Open-ended:

- What parts of this task tend to feel stressful, draining, or easy to avoid?
- When this task goes wrong, what do you usually blame: yourself, the product, the situation, or something else?
- What helps you feel in control while completing this task?
- What kind of feedback makes you feel successful without feeling patronized?
- What would make this experience feel safer, calmer, or easier to restart after stepping away?

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I avoid this kind of task when I feel stressed or overwhelmed.
- Clear progress and small confirmations help me continue.
- I need control over interruptions while doing this task.
- I want settings or support to appear within the flow, not only in a separate settings area.
- I am more willing to continue when the product confirms what I have completed.

Selection guidance: avoid asking for diagnoses unless required. Recruit for emotional impact and cognitive demand instead.

### AI, Automation, and Personalization

Use when the experience includes AI assistants, recommendations, personalization, summarization, writing help, prediction, automation, or adaptive guidance.

Open-ended:

- When would you trust an automated suggestion in this task? When would you not trust it?
- What would you want to fact-check before using an AI-generated answer, summary, or draft?
- What should an AI assistant ask before using your data or adapting to you?
- What would make an AI suggestion feel helpful rather than intrusive?
- What mistakes from an AI system would feel minor, and what mistakes would feel harmful?

Scale statements, 1 strongly disagree to 7 strongly agree:

- I want AI tools to explain what information they used when the answer matters.
- I want to stay in control of whether AI changes my work.
- I am comfortable correcting AI if it helps improve my experience.
- I worry that personalization will make assumptions about me.
- I want recommendations to help me discover alternatives, not only repeat what I already chose.

Selection guidance: include people with different privacy comfort levels, trust levels, correction willingness, and experiences with being misrepresented by automated systems.

### Device, Assistive Technology, and Configuration

Use when the experience must work across devices, input methods, physical contexts, adaptive accessories, setup, installation, product-to-web flows, or assistive technology.

Open-ended:

- What devices, accessories, assistive technologies, browser settings, or physical setup do you use for this task?
- What parts of your setup are essential, even if other people might not notice them?
- What configuration took effort to discover or maintain?
- What would help someone else understand how to make a similar setup work?
- Where does the product assume a standard mouse, keyboard, screen, posture, hand use, vision, hearing, or network connection?

Selection guidance: include participants who use custom configurations and participants who are unaware of available accessibility settings, because awareness and setup are part of the product experience.

## Activity Card Workflow

The activity workflow has five phases. Use it linearly for a full design cycle, or select activities for the current decision.

### Phase 1: Get Oriented

Use to build empathy through evidence, not assumption.

#### Computer Trust

Purpose: reveal why people trust or mistrust technology.

How to facilitate:

1. Write: `I would trust a computer to ___, but I would only trust a human to ___.`
2. Ask participants or team members to fill the blanks as many times as possible in five minutes.
3. Discuss what stands out, what forces affect trust, and how technology could behave better.

Use when designing AI, automation, recommendations, account flows, privacy choices, or high-consequence decisions.

#### Human-to-Computer Role-Play

Purpose: reveal where technology fails to match helpful human interaction.

How to facilitate:

1. Choose a human interaction such as ordering coffee, making a return, asking for directions, booking an appointment, or resolving an account problem.
2. Role-play the human-to-human version.
3. Note verbal cues, nonverbal cues, emotion, timing, repair, and trust-building behavior.
4. Repeat with one person playing the computer or product.
5. Discuss where communication broke down and what the human interaction teaches the product.

Use when designing conversational UI, AI assistants, onboarding, support, errors, coaching, or notification behavior.

#### Learn from the Experts

Purpose: learn from people who experience disability or exclusion on a permanent or recurring basis.

How to facilitate:

1. Prepare with accessibility sensitivity training or equivalent moderator preparation.
2. Interview people with varied abilities, contexts, and adaptations.
3. Ask about strengths, motivations, daily tasks, permanent/temporary/situational parallels, interaction challenges, and the best way to understand their day-to-day experience with people and technology.

Use before framing the design challenge. Do not use this as extraction without follow-up; participants should see how their input influenced the work.

#### Capture Research Insights

Purpose: turn notes into a focused design challenge.

Synthesis prompts:

- With human capabilities and motivations in mind, what strongest themes appeared?
- What mismatches appeared in human-to-technology interaction?
- What were the top methods of access: touch, sight, hearing, voice, keyboard, switch, speech input, screen reader, captions, or other?
- What human-to-human interaction should inform product behavior?
- What design challenge should be solved?
- What need does it address?
- Why does it matter to the audience?

Use after interviews, observation, support-ticket review, or prototype sessions.

### Phase 2: Frame

Use to model the person, context, and ecosystem around the task.

#### Create a Persona Spectrum

Purpose: connect permanent, temporary, and situational mismatches.

How to facilitate:

1. Start with a person who has a permanent limit to at least one ability or recurring need.
2. Ask what they like to do and how they do it.
3. Note situations where they experience friction or limited access.
4. Map related temporary and situational scenarios.

Example spectrum structure:

| Ability area | Permanent | Temporary | Situational |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Touch | One arm | Arm injury | Holding a baby |
| See | Blindness | Cataract or migraine | Bright sunlight or distracted driving |
| Hear | Deafness | Ear infection | Loud crowd |
| Speak | Non-speaking | Laryngitis | Heavy accent or noisy environment |

Use this to scale insights without flattening individual needs.

#### Persona Network

Purpose: understand the person’s ecosystem.

How to facilitate:

1. Pick a participant or persona.
2. Map 3-5 people they interact with often: family, coworkers, friends, strangers, caregivers, interpreters, support agents, managers, classmates, or community members.
3. Mark who they rely on, trust, enjoy, avoid, ask for help, or coordinate with.
4. List mismatches between the person, their environment, and the network.

Use when the experience depends on collaboration, support, handoff, social pressure, caregiving, accountability, privacy, or shared decisions.

#### Interaction Diary

Purpose: learn from observation of real interactions.

How to facilitate:

1. Select a location or remote context where people interact with each other, objects, and technology.
2. Observe carefully and take notes or sketches over time.
3. Capture verbal cues, nonverbal cues, movement, body language, pauses, frustration, workarounds, and moments of success.
4. Compare human-to-human, human-to-object, and human-to-technology interactions.

Use before redesigning navigation, physical-digital flows, support experiences, service interactions, or device setup.

#### Human Analogy

Purpose: clarify the role technology should play.

How to facilitate:

1. Brainstorm the human equivalent of the technology: assistant, teacher, coach, librarian, concierge, analyst, translator, guide, editor, scheduler, or guardrail.
2. Interview people who perform that role well.
3. Identify what makes them effective: timing, tone, restraint, explanation, repair, confidence, boundaries, or memory.
4. Translate those behaviors into product behavior.

Use when designing AI, onboarding, alerts, guidance, coaching, support, or automation.

### Phase 3: Ideate

Use to convert mismatches into design opportunities and concepts.

#### Mismatch to Solution I

Purpose: turn research findings into opportunity questions.

How to facilitate:

1. List every mismatched interaction identified in research.
2. Convert each mismatch into a focused question:
   - `How might we create...`
   - `How might we improve...`
   - `How might we enable...`
   - `How might we reduce...`
   - `How might we preserve...`
3. Select the three opportunities worth exploring first.

Facilitation note: work one mismatch at a time to prevent overwhelm.

#### Mismatch to Solution II

Purpose: generate concepts from selected opportunity questions.

How to facilitate:

1. Choose three opportunities from Mismatch to Solution I.
2. Individually brainstorm for 3-5 minutes per opportunity.
3. Write one idea per note.
4. Share, cluster, and filter as a group.
5. Keep unusual workarounds; they often point to a better pattern.

Facilitation note: prioritize idea volume before critique.

#### Design a Microinteraction

Purpose: expose inclusion risks in small sequences.

How to facilitate:

1. Choose a specific interaction from the concept or prototype.
2. Map whether it is user-initiated or system-initiated.
3. Map the trigger.
4. Map how feedback begins.
5. Map how the person interacts with feedback.
6. Map what happens immediately after feedback completes.
7. Mark where focus, recall, decision-making, communication, access, and control can fail.

Use for alerts, toasts, AI suggestions, modal flows, form errors, loading states, confirmations, undo, settings, and handoffs.

#### Evaluate Technology’s Role

Purpose: test whether the chosen technology is appropriate and simple enough.

How to facilitate:

1. Select a concept or prototype.
2. Name the role technology plays: collect and summarize, translate, transport, listen, remind, suggest, coach, protect, store, compare, or decide.
3. Ask whether that role is the simplest appropriate way to achieve the outcome.
4. Remove technology that increases cognitive, emotional, physical, social, or privacy demand without clear benefit.

Use especially before adding AI, automation, personalization, complex settings, or new devices.

### Phase 4: Iterate

Use to test quickly and learn from delight and pain points.

#### Low-Fidelity Prototype

Purpose: refine ideas through fast, low-cost, participant-centered testing.

How to facilitate:

1. List microinteractions in the design.
2. Choose one interaction to prototype.
3. Use paper, sketching, clickable wireframes, role-play, recorded sounds, rough video, components, or lightweight code.
4. Ensure the prototype communicates its own function without heavy explanation.
5. Test with participants and observe both delight and pain.
6. Revise immediately when possible.

Facilitation note: a person may role-play the technology with a prepared script. This is useful for AI or assistant behavior before building it.

#### Simulations

Purpose: reveal prototype improvements by applying temporary and situational limitations.

How to facilitate:

1. Write the steps a person takes in the solution.
2. Choose a temporary or situational limitation.
3. Recreate or approximate that limitation carefully.
4. Walk through the steps.
5. Note what could be improved.
6. Adjust the design.
7. Repeat with another limitation.

Use simulations as a stress test, not as a replacement for disabled participants.

### Phase 5: Optimize

Use to test adaptation across contexts and after assumptions have changed.

#### Context and Capability Match

Purpose: evaluate whether a concept adapts when environment changes capability.

How to facilitate:

1. Choose a physical or social context.
2. Choose a temporary or situational limitation.
3. Ask how well the solution adapts to that combination.
4. List modifications needed.
5. Revise the scenario to include the adaptation.
6. Repeat with other combinations.

Use when designing responsive web flows, setup, account creation, notifications, mobile use, low bandwidth, product-to-web journeys, and assistive technology support.

#### Situational Adaptation

Purpose: discover ways the solution can work across situational limitations.

How to facilitate:

1. Choose one physical context, one social context, and one time-of-day condition.
2. Take 3-5 minutes to list possible limitations in that combined scenario.
3. Brainstorm how the solution can adapt.
4. Revise the solution.
5. Repeat with a different combination.

Use with existing solutions to uncover where exclusion has already been designed in.

## Facilitation Scripts

### Opening Script

`Today we are here to learn from your experience. You are not here to validate our assumptions or perform the product correctly. If something is confusing, stressful, inaccessible, too fast, too vague, or not worth your effort, that is useful information. You can pause, skip, ask for clarification, or stop at any time.`

### Access Check

`Before we begin, is there anything about the format, timing, materials, audio, captions, display, input method, breaks, or pace that would make this session easier for you?`

### Consent and Privacy Check

`We will use what we learn to improve the product. We will not include your name or identifying details in readouts unless you explicitly agree. Some topics may involve personal workarounds or sensitive experiences. You can choose how much to share.`

For AI or personalization studies, add:

`This session may discuss data, personalization, recommendations, or automated suggestions. We will be explicit about what is recorded, what is not, and whether anything is used to train or improve a system.`

### Participant Story Prompt

`Think about the last time you tried to complete this task or something like it. What were you trying to accomplish, what made it easier, what got in the way, and what did you do to work around it?`

### Adaptation Prompt

`When the product does not support you well, what do you change about your environment, tools, timing, device, settings, process, or support network to make it work?`

### Motivation Prompt

`Why does this task matter to you beyond simply finishing the steps? What larger goal or motivation is it connected to?`

### Cognitive Demand Prompts

Learning:

- `How did you figure out what to do first?`
- `Where would you expect help to be?`
- `Would you rather explore first, follow a guide, or have structured support?`

Focus:

- `What pulled attention away from the task?`
- `Which interruptions were useful, and which were costly?`
- `What would help you tune in or tune out?`

Decision-making:

- `What information did you need before acting?`
- `Were consequences and tradeoffs clear?`
- `Did any choice feel risky, irreversible, or overloaded?`

Recall:

- `What did you have to remember from a previous step or session?`
- `Where would you expect the product to preserve state or remind you?`
- `What would help you resume after interruption?`

Communication:

- `Was the tone right for the moment?`
- `Would another format, timing, or modality work better?`
- `What should the system learn about your preferences, and what should it ask first?`

### Mental Health and Emotional Safety Prompts

- `Where did the experience make you feel in control?`
- `Where did it make you feel lost, blamed, rushed, watched, or uncertain?`
- `What would help you feel successful sooner?`
- `What would make it easier to come back after stepping away?`
- `What should the product avoid doing when someone is stressed or overwhelmed?`

### Prototype Testing Prompts

- `What do you think this screen or object is asking you to do?`
- `What would you do next?`
- `What are you unsure about?`
- `What would you expect to happen if you choose this action?`
- `Where would you go for help?`
- `What would make this easier with your actual setup?`
- `What part feels unnecessary, intrusive, or too much?`
- `What part worked better than expected?`

### Closing Script

`We will synthesize what we heard, identify product decisions affected by it, and share the changes or open questions that come from this session when possible. Is there anything we missed, misinterpreted, or should handle with extra care?`

## Prototype Test Plan

Use this plan when the team already has a wireframe, low-fidelity prototype, clickable prototype, or working build.

1. State the decision the test must inform.
2. Recruit by relevant mismatch and cognitive demand.
3. Test with participants' real or realistic devices, settings, and assistive technologies when possible.
4. Include at least one interruption or resume scenario if recall or focus is relevant.
5. Include at least one error, loading, confirmation, or undo state if the task includes risk.
6. Include contextual settings if personalization, notifications, appearance, privacy, or guidance are relevant.
7. Observe behaviors, not only opinions.
8. Ask how the experience made the person feel and whether it preserved control.
9. Record adaptations and workarounds as design inputs.
10. End by asking what should change before they would use it in real life.

## Research for Specific Product Surfaces

### AI Assistants and Automation

Research questions:

- Where can AI reduce task initiation, recall, communication, or decision demand?
- Where can AI increase anxiety, bias, mistrust, or loss of control?
- What references, citations, previews, or explanations are needed before use?
- When should AI suggest, draft, coach, summarize, ask, or stay quiet?
- What should be editable, reversible, dismissible, or opt-in?
- What privacy and consent touchpoints are needed across the journey?
- Does personalization reinforce narrow assumptions or help people discover alternatives?

Facilitation activity: combine Computer Trust, Human Analogy, Design a Microinteraction, and Evaluate Technology’s Role.

### Notifications, Suggestions, and Alerts

Research questions:

- What messages require full, partial, or peripheral attention?
- What is the cost if the person misses the message?
- What is the cost if the message interrupts them?
- What mode should the message use: visual, audio, haptic, inline, summary, badge, toast, modal, email, or none?
- What should be batched, delayed, silenced, or allowed to break through?
- How should the system adapt to behavior and context without assuming too much?

Facilitation activity: use Focus screener questions, Human-to-Computer Role-Play, Design a Microinteraction, and Context and Capability Match.

### Onboarding and Guidance

Research questions:

- Do people want trial-and-error, semi-structured guidance, or structured support?
- Does desire for guidance change with confidence, motivation, stakes, or context?
- Where should help appear, and when should the person be able to skip it?
- Can the person revisit guidance later?
- Does guidance include context such as time, effort, prerequisites, risk, and end state?
- Can people ask questions in their own words?

Facilitation activity: use Learn screener questions, neutral learning scenarios such as cooking, and Low-Fidelity Prototype.

### Forms, Wizards, and Multi-Step Flows

Research questions:

- What must the person remember from step to step?
- Where is progress visible?
- Are back, save, undo, pause, resume, and edit available?
- Which choices feel high-stakes?
- Where do errors blame the person or hide the next step?
- Are settings and support surfaced in the flow?

Facilitation activity: use Decision-Making and Recall screeners, Design a Microinteraction, and prototype testing with interruption/resume.

### Collections, Dashboards, and Accumulated Data

Research questions:

- How do people search, filter, sort, group, collapse, or prioritize information?
- What information is essential versus noise?
- What visual hierarchy supports scanning?
- What must be narrated, resized, highlighted, or summarized?
- What does the person do when overwhelmed?

Facilitation activity: Interaction Diary, Capture Research Insights, and Low-Fidelity Prototype.

### Product-to-Web or Device-Adjacent Experiences

Research questions:

- What physical setup, device configuration, assistive technology, or accessory is part of the task?
- What instructions or demonstrations are needed before the person can start?
- What settings or configurations should be captured and reusable?
- Where does the web flow assume a standard body, device, input method, display, posture, or environment?
- How can the team publish examples that show disabled users succeeding with the product?

Facilitation activity: Learn from the Experts, Interaction Diary, Context and Capability Match, and Situational Adaptation.

## Synthesis Method

Synthesize immediately after sessions while context is fresh.

### 1. Extract Evidence

Capture:

- Direct quotes
- Observed behaviors
- Workarounds and adaptations
- Emotional cues
- Missed expectations
- Access barriers
- Moments of delight
- Moments of control or loss of control
- Assistive technology or configuration details
- Participant corrections to team assumptions

### 2. Convert Evidence to Mismatches

Use this format:

`Participant evidence: [quote/behavior/adaptation].`

`Mismatch: the product assumes [ability/context/memory/confidence/control] but the person needs [support/flexibility/control/alternative].`

`Design implication: change [surface/flow/component/content/system behavior] to [proposed direction].`

### 3. Cluster Mismatches

Cluster by:

- Cognitive demand: learn, focus, decision-making, recall, communication
- Emotional demand: stress, confidence, self-blame, privacy concern, overwhelm
- Access method: keyboard, touch, screen reader, captions, voice, switch, pointer, magnification, high contrast
- Journey moment: discovery, start, progress, error, waiting, confirmation, resume, support, completion
- Product behavior: guidance, alert, setting, AI, form, content, navigation, data display

### 4. Prioritize

Prioritize with these criteria:

- Severity: how much the mismatch blocks participation or causes harm
- Frequency: how often the mismatch appears across participants or contexts
- Reach: how many permanent, temporary, and situational cases it can affect
- Reversibility: whether people can recover from the problem
- Control: whether the person can adapt or override the system
- Feasibility: what can be changed now versus later
- Trust impact: whether the mismatch affects privacy, safety, dignity, or belief in the product

### 5. Write Opportunity Questions

Examples:

- `How might we help people resume after an interruption without requiring memory of prior steps?`
- `How might we let suggestions remain discoverable without taking full attention?`
- `How might we make guidance available without forcing a single learning path?`
- `How might we show consequences before action without overwhelming people with details?`
- `How might we let AI personalize communication only after consent and correction?`

### 6. Produce the Decision Log

For each decision, record:

- Research evidence
- Mismatch addressed
- Product change
- Owner
- Acceptance criteria
- Open risk
- Follow-up participant validation needed

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- Recruiting only people who match the team’s demographics or technical familiarity
- Recruiting by diagnosis when the product decision depends on a need spectrum
- Treating disabled participants as final validators instead of early co-creators
- Running a lab-only study when real context is the source of the mismatch
- Asking participants to reveal sensitive medical details without need
- Using simulations as evidence of lived experience
- Defending the design during research
- Measuring only task completion while ignoring control, confidence, emotion, and recovery
- Treating silence or politeness as success
- Ignoring adaptations because they are not part of the intended flow
- Failing to tell participants what changed because of their input
- Adding AI, personalization, or automation without researching trust, consent, bias, correction, and control

## Preflight Checklist

Before running research or co-creation, verify:

- The research objective is tied to a product decision.
- Motivation, goal, task, cognitive demand, and emotional demand are explicit.
- Participants are recruited by need spectrum and context, not diagnosis alone.
- The recruit includes demographic and lived-experience diversity.
- A pre-session access and fit check is planned.
- The session has clear consent, privacy, recording, and compensation language.
- Materials are accessible and available in advance when helpful.
- Facilitation allows verbal, written, demonstrated, and asynchronous contribution where possible.
- Breaks, pacing, and opt-out paths are built in.
- Observers know not to defend or debate.
- Activities are selected for the current decision, not used as decoration.
- Prototype testing includes states that matter: error, loading, confirmation, interruption, resume, settings, and support.
- Synthesis will capture quotes, behaviors, adaptations, mismatches, emotions, and product implications.
- A feedback loop will tell participants or stakeholders what changed.
